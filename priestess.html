VIII · The High Priestess of the Microscope

Co-Investigator · whole task · mixed-initiated
Tarot · High Priestess Instrument · Microscope Scope: whole Initiative: mixed Survey's central card
"Insimul investigamus" — "Together we inquire."

What kind of AI agent

The project has run for half a year, and the AI has been there the whole arc — not waiting for instructions, not running off on its own. It proposes the next experiment; you reframe it. It interprets a result; you push back. You set the direction, and it genuinely helps carry the inquiry.

That is the High Priestess — the card the term "AI co-scientist" was coined to describe. You pursue whole research projects jointly with AI as a research partner, both of you initiating moves across a long arc: you set direction, the system proposes experiments and interpretations, you intervene at every fork. Whole scope, shared initiative. It is the survey's aspirational centre — and what it asks for is reconstructable joint authorship: every decision loggable as yours, the system's, or shared, because publication ethics depend on it.

The Tarot archetype

The High Priestess sits between two pillars, holding the scroll of the unwritten Torah. She is the card of hidden knowledge approached jointly — the gateway between the seen and the unseen. The Priestess does not reveal alone; she sits with the seeker.

The scientific instrument

The compound microscope made the unseen seeable, founding modern life sciences (van Leeuwenhoek, 1670s). The microscope is the canonical joint inquiry instrument: two parties — the slide and the eyepiece — must align for the object to be revealed.

Where to start

  • Treat the purpose-built systems as experiments. Google's AI Co-Scientist, supervised Agent Laboratory, lab agents with audit trails — these are research artifacts, not dependable products yet. Watch and trial them; do not build on them.
  • Practise the pattern without the full system. Run a long project with a general assistant, but keep a shared notebook where every substantive decision is logged with who made it — you, the tool, or both.
  • Decide your authorship rule before you need it. If a system genuinely co-drove a result, work out now how that gets attributed and disclosed — do not leave it for a reviewer to ask.

Strength

The dream — joint goal-pursuit at full project scope, human-AI alignment maintained over time.

Signature failure mode

The audit problem. Hard to know who decided what; reproducibility nightmare; publication ethics genuinely unclear. The pilot's Q11 reproducibility-and-provenance cluster reads as a defensive response to this cell's risk.

What's at stake

The High Priestess is a partnership of indefinite duration — a counterpart you inquire alongside, not a tool you pick up and put down. Taking the system seriously as a partner means it cannot simply be erased into the methods section: if it genuinely co-drove the work, it has to be attributable, the way a collaborator is. That is the unresolved part. Science has rules for human authorship and rules for tools, and a genuine AI co-investigator falls between them — which is exactly the question this card is named to provoke.

Background reading — Buber on the I–Thou partnership extended over a long arc; Latour on the symmetry between human and non-human actors — a co-investigator that must remain attributable in the literature, not erased into a tool.

How you got here

The survey routes you to this card based on the following signals:

  • Q3 leans whole-task — experiment design, reproducing papers, manuscript support, often combined with broad operations.
  • Q11 balances "Reproducibility" and "Citations/provenance" with "Dataset understanding" — both human and system contribute substantively.
  • Q4 frustrations are nuanced: "misunderstanding dataset" + "overconfident" — the respondent wants joint inquiry with built-in audit.

A snapshot, not a verdict. This card reflects how you work with AI tools right now — which is shaped as much by what you have had access to as by what you would choose. The High Priestess is the survey's aspirational centre: for almost everyone it describes a direction of travel rather than a current practice. Read it as where the field is pointed.
